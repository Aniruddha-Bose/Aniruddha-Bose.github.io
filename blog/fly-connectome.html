166,000 Neurons, 125 Million Connections: Mapping a Fly’s Brain with AI

How AI helped scientists map an entire nervous system.

Neuroscience AI Biology Connectomics
166,691Neurons Mapped
~125MSynapses
11,691Neuron Types
100+Years of Fly Research

What if you could trace every wire inside a brain?

On September 3, 2026, Google Research and its scientific partners announced a remarkable achievement: researchers had created a complete map of the brain and central nervous system of an adult male fruit fly.

The map contains more than 166,000 neurons connected through approximately 125 million synapses.

And artificial intelligence played a major role in making it possible.

The project was a collaboration involving Google Research, HHMI Janelia Research Campus, researchers in Cambridge, U.K., and other scientists. Together, they produced what Google described as the largest brain map by number of neurons created so far.

But why spend years mapping something as tiny as a fruit fly?

The answer could help us understand one of the most complicated machines we know of: the brain.

The Basics

First, What Exactly Did They Map?

A brain isn’t simply a collection of neurons.

What really matters is how those neurons are connected.

Image: Thomas Splettstoesser (scistyle.com), CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons

Neurons communicate with other neurons through connections called synapses. Signals travelling through these networks allow an animal to see, smell, move, react and behave.

Imagine a huge city. If every neuron were a building, the synapses would be the roads connecting those buildings.

Knowing where every building is wouldn’t tell you how the city works. You would also need to know where the roads go.

A map showing these neural connections is called a connectome.

The new male fruit-fly connectome contains 166,691 neurons and thousands of different neuron types.

It gives scientists an incredibly detailed wiring diagram of how different parts of the fly’s nervous system connect.

Beyond the Brain

It Isn’t Just a Brain Map

One thing makes this project particularly interesting.

The researchers didn’t stop at the brain.

The map also includes the fly’s ventral nerve cord, which performs some functions similar to the spinal cord in vertebrates.

That means scientists can investigate pathways that extend from sensory information all the way toward movement. For example:

  1. The fly sees something
  2. Visual neurons process it
  3. Signals travel through other circuits
  4. Motor neurons activate
  5. The fly moves

Scientists can now trace pathways like these through the connectome.

That makes the project more than a static picture of a brain.

It provides a map connecting sensing to action.

The Method

How Do You Map Something This Complicated?

You can’t simply put a fly underneath a normal microscope and photograph its entire nervous system.

Instead, researchers used electron microscopy.

Extremely thin sections of the nervous system were imaged at very high resolution.

This produced millions of two-dimensional images.

But that created another problem.

Somehow, all those individual images had to be turned back into the three-dimensional structures of neurons.

Try to Imagine This

Imagine slicing an enormous bowl of tangled spaghetti into millions of incredibly thin layers. Now imagine being given photographs of every layer and being asked to reconstruct every individual strand of spaghetti from beginning to end. That gives you some idea of the challenge.

The AI

This Is Where AI Comes In

Reconstructing such an enormous dataset manually would be extraordinarily difficult.

Google Research developed computational and AI-based techniques that helped reconstruct neurons from the electron-microscope images.

The AI could identify structures across the images and help transform the enormous collection of 2D slices into three-dimensional neural shapes.

But the process wasn’t completely automatic.

Human experts at HHMI Janelia then carefully reviewed, corrected and annotated the reconstruction.

This combination was essential:

  1. Microscopy produced the images
  2. AI helped reconstruct the neurons
  3. Human experts verified the results

It is a great example of AI being used not to replace scientists, but to help them work with an amount of data that would otherwise be extremely difficult to process.

The Animal

Why a Fruit Fly?

At first, a fruit fly might seem like an odd animal to study.

Why not map something more similar to humans?

The answer is complexity.

Image: André Karwath, CC BY-SA 2.5, via Wikimedia Commons

Fruit flies are relatively simple compared with humans, but their nervous systems are still capable of producing surprisingly sophisticated behaviour.

They can see and follow objects, detect smells and tastes, navigate their environment, fly, walk, search for food, react to danger, interact with other flies and perform courtship behaviours.

Scientists have also studied the fruit fly, Drosophila melanogaster, for more than a century.

That makes it one of biology’s most important model organisms — an organism scientists study to discover principles that may apply more broadly.

The fruit fly gives researchers something complicated enough to be interesting but small enough that mapping its entire nervous system is actually possible.

The Comparison

Male vs Female Brains

There is another reason this particular map is useful.

Scientists already have detailed connectomes of female fruit flies.

Now they can compare them.

Researchers found that much of the nervous system is similar between males and females, but some neurons and connections differ.

The published male connectome contains 11,691 identified neuron types. Researchers reported thousands of types shared between the sexes, alongside neuron types that were male-specific, female-specific or structurally different.

These differences are particularly interesting because male and female flies display different behaviours.

Instead of studying these behaviours only from the outside by watching what flies do, researchers can now investigate the actual circuits inside their nervous systems.

The Pathways

From Seeing to Moving

One of the most fascinating possibilities is tracing information through the nervous system.

A fly’s eyes contain neurons that detect visual information. Those neurons connect to other neurons. Those connect to still more neurons. Eventually, some pathways reach motor neurons responsible for controlling movement.

With a complete connectome, researchers can begin tracing these chains.

Google highlighted an example involving a visual pathway that ultimately reaches a motor neuron. Some of the intermediate circuitry is associated with male-specific courtship behaviour.

Suddenly, a behaviour we can observe from the outside can be investigated as a physical network of connected cells.

That is one of the reasons connectomics is so powerful.

The Limits

Does Mapping the Brain Mean We Understand It?

Not quite.

A complete wiring diagram is an enormous achievement, but it doesn’t automatically explain how the brain works.

Think of It Like This

Imagine finding the circuit diagram for an incredibly complicated computer. You could see every component and every connection. But you would still need to determine what signals were travelling through those circuits and what each part was doing.

The same problem exists with a connectome.

Scientists now know an extraordinary amount about where the connections are.

The next challenge is understanding what happens inside those connections when the animal is actually behaving.

So this map isn’t the end of the research. It is a foundation for what comes next.

The Future

Could We Do This with a Human Brain?

This is where the scale becomes almost unbelievable.

The fruit fly nervous system mapped in this project contains roughly 166,000 neurons. The human brain contains tens of billions. And every neuron can form connections with many others.

So although mapping a fly is incredibly difficult, mapping an entire human brain at the same level of detail would be an enormously larger challenge.

Researchers are gradually working on larger nervous systems and brain samples, however.

The techniques developed through projects like this — better microscopy, larger computing systems and improved AI reconstruction — could help scientists tackle increasingly complicated brains.

The fly is therefore not necessarily the destination. It is one step along the way.

Why I Think This Is So Interesting

The most fascinating part of this project isn’t simply that scientists created a huge dataset.

It’s how they did it. This project combines several completely different fields:

  • Biology provided the questions.
  • Electron microscopy produced the images.
  • Computer science handled the enormous datasets.
  • Artificial intelligence helped reconstruct the neurons.
  • Human scientists verified and interpreted the results.

None of those pieces alone would have produced the finished connectome.

Together, they created something that would have been extremely difficult to imagine only a few decades ago.

Closing Thoughts

A fruit fly is something most of us would barely notice.

Yet inside its tiny nervous system are more than 166,000 neurons connected through around 125 million synapses.

Those connections allow it to see, smell, move, navigate and interact with the world around it.

Now, for the first time, scientists have a complete map of those connections in an adult male fruit fly’s brain and central nervous system.

The map doesn’t mean we suddenly understand everything happening inside a brain.

But it gives researchers something incredibly important: the wiring diagram.

And perhaps the most exciting part is that AI (technology inspired, however loosely, by our understanding of biological neural networks) is now helping scientists investigate biological neural networks themselves.

Keep Exploring

We built AI partly by taking inspiration from brains. Now AI is helping us understand them.
