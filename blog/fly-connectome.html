166,000 Neurons, 125 Million Connections: Mapping a Fly’s Brain with AI

How AI helped scientists map an entire nervous system.

Neuroscience AI Biology Connectomics
166,691Neurons Mapped
~125MSynapses
11,691Neuron Types
100+Years of Fly Research

What if you could trace every single wire inside a brain?

On September 3, 2026, Google Research and its scientific partners announced that they’d made a complete map of the brain and central nervous system of an adult male fruit fly.

The map has more than 166,000 neurons, linked up by about 125 million synapses.

Artificial intelligence played a big part in getting it done.

The project was a collaboration between Google Research, HHMI Janelia Research Campus, researchers in Cambridge, U.K., and other scientists. Google described the result as the largest brain map by number of neurons created so far.

So why would anyone spend years mapping something as tiny as a fruit fly?

Because it might help us figure out the brain, which is one of the most complicated machines we know of.

The Basics

First, What Exactly Did They Map?

A brain is made of neurons, but a list of neurons on its own doesn’t tell you much.

What you really want to know is how they’re connected.

Image: Thomas Splettstoesser (scistyle.com), CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons

Neurons talk to each other through connections called synapses. The signals moving through these networks are what let an animal see, smell, move, react and behave.

I think of it like a big city. If every neuron were a building, the synapses would be the roads between them.

Knowing where every building is wouldn’t tell you how the city works. You’d also need to know where the roads go.

A map of all these connections is called a connectome.

The new male fruit-fly connectome has 166,691 neurons and thousands of different neuron types.

So scientists now have a really detailed wiring diagram of how the different parts of the fly’s nervous system link up.

Beyond the Brain

The Nerve Cord Is in There Too

There’s one detail about this project that I especially like.

The researchers didn’t only map the brain.

The map also covers the fly’s ventral nerve cord, which does some of the same jobs as the spinal cord in vertebrates.

That means scientists can follow pathways all the way from sensory information toward movement. Something like this:

  1. The fly sees something
  2. Visual neurons process it
  3. Signals travel through other circuits
  4. Motor neurons activate
  5. The fly moves

With the connectome, scientists can now trace pathways like these step by step.

That makes it a lot more useful than a still picture of a brain would be.

It actually links sensing to action.

The Method

How Do You Map Something This Complicated?

You can’t just put a fly under a normal microscope and photograph its whole nervous system.

So the researchers used electron microscopy instead.

They imaged extremely thin sections of the nervous system at very high resolution.

That gave them millions of two-dimensional images.

Which left them with a new problem, though.

All those separate images somehow had to be turned back into the three-dimensional shapes of the neurons.

How I Picture It

Say you sliced a giant bowl of tangled spaghetti into millions of super thin layers. Then someone hands you a photo of every layer and asks you to rebuild each strand of spaghetti from one end to the other. That’s roughly what the challenge was like.

The AI

Where the AI Comes In

Rebuilding a dataset this big by hand would be extremely hard.

Google Research developed computational and AI-based techniques that helped reconstruct neurons from the electron-microscope images.

The AI could pick out structures across the images and help turn the huge stack of 2D slices into 3D neuron shapes.

The process wasn’t fully automatic, though.

Human experts at HHMI Janelia then went through the reconstruction carefully, fixing mistakes and adding annotations.

Each step depended on the others.

  1. Microscopy produced the images
  2. AI helped reconstruct the neurons
  3. Human experts verified the results

I like this as an example of AI in science. The scientists were still the ones doing the science, and the AI helped them deal with an amount of data that would otherwise be extremely difficult to process.

The Animal

Why a Fruit Fly?

A fruit fly might seem like a strange animal to pick at first.

Why not map something closer to humans?

Mostly it comes down to complexity.

Image: André Karwath, CC BY-SA 2.5, via Wikimedia Commons

Compared with humans, fruit flies are fairly simple, but their nervous systems can still produce surprisingly sophisticated behaviour.

They can see and follow objects, detect smells and tastes, find their way around, fly, walk, search for food, react to danger, interact with other flies and do courtship behaviours.

Scientists have also been studying the fruit fly, Drosophila melanogaster, for more than a century.

That’s made it one of biology’s most important model organisms. A model organism is one that scientists study to find principles that might apply more broadly.

A fruit fly is complicated enough to be interesting, but small enough that mapping its whole nervous system is actually doable.

The Comparison

Male vs Female Brains

This particular map is useful for another reason too.

Scientists already have detailed connectomes of female fruit flies.

So now they can compare the two.

Researchers found that a lot of the nervous system is similar in males and females, but some neurons and connections are different.

The published male connectome has 11,691 identified neuron types. Researchers reported thousands of types shared between the sexes, plus neuron types that were male-specific, female-specific or structurally different.

I find those differences really interesting, because male and female flies behave differently.

Instead of only studying these behaviours from the outside by watching what flies do, researchers can now look at the actual circuits inside their nervous systems.

The Pathways

From Seeing to Moving

One of the coolest things you can do with a connectome is follow information as it moves through the nervous system.

A fly’s eyes contain neurons that detect visual information. Those neurons connect to other neurons, which connect to even more neurons, and eventually some of these pathways reach the motor neurons that control movement.

With a complete connectome, researchers can start tracing these chains.

Google highlighted one example where a visual pathway ends up at a motor neuron. Some of the circuitry in between is linked to male-specific courtship behaviour.

So a behaviour we can watch from the outside can now be studied as a physical network of connected cells.

That’s a big part of why connectomics is so powerful.

The Limits

Does Mapping the Brain Mean We Understand It?

Not quite, unfortunately.

Having the complete wiring diagram is a huge achievement, but it doesn’t automatically explain how the brain works.

A Computer Comparison

Say you found the circuit diagram for a really complicated computer. You’d be able to see every component and every connection, but you’d still have to work out what signals were running through those circuits and what each part was actually doing.

A connectome has the same problem.

Scientists now know a huge amount about where the connections are.

The next step is figuring out what happens inside those connections when the animal is actually behaving.

So this map is really a foundation for whatever research comes next.

The Future

Could We Do This with a Human Brain?

This is where the numbers get kind of ridiculous.

The fruit fly nervous system mapped in this project has roughly 166,000 neurons. The human brain has tens of billions, and every neuron can connect to lots of others.

Mapping a fly was already really hard. Mapping a whole human brain at the same level of detail would be a far, far bigger job.

Still, researchers are slowly working their way up to larger nervous systems and brain samples.

The techniques built for projects like this could help scientists take on more and more complicated brains. That includes better microscopy, bigger computing systems and improved AI reconstruction.

So the fly might just be one step along the way.

Why I Find This So Interesting

Sure, the dataset is huge.

What I find most interesting, though, is how they did it. The project pulls together a bunch of completely different fields:

  • Biology provided the questions.
  • Electron microscopy produced the images.
  • Computer science handled the enormous datasets.
  • Artificial intelligence helped reconstruct the neurons.
  • Human scientists verified and interpreted the results.

No one of those on its own could have produced the finished connectome.

Put together, they made something that would have been really hard to even imagine a few decades ago.

Closing Thoughts

Most of us would barely notice a fruit fly.

But inside its tiny nervous system there are more than 166,000 neurons, connected by around 125 million synapses.

Those connections let it see, smell, move, navigate and interact with the world around it.

And now, for the first time, scientists have a complete map of those connections in an adult male fruit fly’s brain and central nervous system.

That doesn’t mean we suddenly understand everything going on inside a brain.

It does give researchers something really important, though, which is the wiring diagram.

The part I find most exciting is that AI (technology inspired, however loosely, by our understanding of biological neural networks) is now helping scientists study biological neural networks themselves.

Want More?

We built AI partly by borrowing ideas from brains, and now it’s helping us understand them.
